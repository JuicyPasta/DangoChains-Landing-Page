<h1>The Japanese alphabet, explained</h1>
<p>Japanese doesn't have one alphabet. Everyday writing mixes three scripts, often in the same sentence, and a fourth (Latin letters) shows up on signs and keyboards.</p>

<h2>All three in one sentence</h2>
<p lang="ja" style="font-size:1.4em">私はコーヒーを飲みます。</p>
<p><i>Watashi wa kōhī o nomimasu</i>: "I drink coffee." <span lang="ja">私</span> (I) and <span lang="ja">飲</span> (drink) are kanji; <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> is katakana because it is a borrowed word; <span lang="ja">は</span>, <span lang="ja">を</span> and the verb ending <span lang="ja">みます</span> are hiragana. Kanji carry the meaning, hiragana carries the grammar, and the mix also shows where words begin, since Japanese doesn't use spaces.</p>
<h2>What to learn first</h2>
<ol><li><b><a href="/hiragana-chart/">Hiragana</a></b> first: every sound in the language, and enough to read anything with furigana. Most learners get through it in one to two weeks.</li><li><b><a href="/katakana-chart/">Katakana</a></b> next: the same sounds, so it goes faster, and it unlocks menus and signs full of borrowed words.</li><li><b>Kanji</b> a few at a time, inside words rather than alone: <a href="/study/jlpt/n5/">JLPT N5 words</a>, <a href="/study/topics/numbers/">numbers</a>, <a href="/study/topics/days-months/">days of the week</a>. A kanji usually has more than one reading; see <a href="/study/topics/kanji-readings/">onyomi vs kunyomi</a>.</li></ol>
<p>Romaji helps at the very start, but drop it early: Japanese readers never see it, and spellings like <i>ō</i> vs <i>ou</i> hide how words are written.</p>
